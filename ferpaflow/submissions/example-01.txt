This week's reading on responsible AI use in classrooms made me rethink an
assumption I came in with — that the main risk of students using AI is cheating.
The author's point that the bigger long-term risk is *students never learning to
struggle productively* hit harder. They use the phrase "outsourcing the
desirable difficulty," and I keep coming back to that.

In my own future classroom (I want to teach high school algebra), I think this
shows up most in homework. If a student photographs every problem and gets a
worked solution, they get the dopamine of a finished assignment without any of
the retrieval practice that actually moves things into long-term memory. So I'd
probably restructure homework: fewer problems, but each one asks for a written
explanation of *why* a step works, not just the answer. AI can produce those too,
of course — but it's at least a higher bar, and it's the kind of thing I can
spot-check in a two-minute conversation.

Where I disagree with the reading a little: the author treats the "AI as a tutor"
use case pretty optimistically, and I'm more skeptical. A tutor who never gets
tired and never judges you sounds great until you remember the tutor is also
confidently wrong sometimes and the student has no way to tell. For a kid who's
already behind, that's not obviously better than no help. I don't have a clean
answer here — maybe the move is teaching students to check AI output against a
worked example, which is a skill in itself. But I'd want to pilot that before I'd
trust it.

One thing I'll take into student teaching: I want a written AI policy on day one,
co-written with the students if possible, so it doesn't feel like a trap.
