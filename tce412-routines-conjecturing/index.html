Routine 1 · Three-act task · Act 1

How many boxes of cookies fit in that trunk?

Too low

A number you know is too small

Too high

A number you know is too big

My estimate

…and the reason why

Play the 20-second video: vimeo.com/157324585 · Dan Meyer, CC-BY, from a Nissan ad

Play the Vimeo video first; don't show the question yet. Ask "What do you notice? What do you wonder?" and collect wonders until someone asks how many boxes fit. THEN show the question on this slide. Too low, too high, estimate with a reason, just as in Estimation 180. Adults have little experience here, so estimates will spread widely. That's good.

Act 2

What information would help?

Cargo space

39.3 cubic feet

One box of cookies

7 × 2.3 × 4.6 inches

Measurements from Nissan and Dan Meyer's task notes

Ask what they'd need to know BEFORE revealing the two cards, and have them estimate those quantities too (Meyer's design choice: he cut this screen from Act 1 so students would ask for it). Then reveal. Watch for the trap: feet vs. inches. 39.3 ft³ × 1,728 = 67,910 in³; one box is 7 × 2.3 × 4.6 ≈ 74.1 in³; 67,910 ÷ 74.1 ≈ 917 boxes if packed perfectly. Anyone who divides 39.3 by 74.1 gets about half a box. A great moment to revoice.

Act 3 · The reveal

How many did Nissan actually fit?

Compare the real count to your math. Why might they differ?

Nissan's reveal video: facebook.com/nissanusa/videos/10156496173410375

Play Nissan's reveal video. CHECK THIS LINK BEFORE CLASS: it's on Facebook and may need a login. I couldn't confirm the real count; the math says about 917 if packed perfectly. The discussion is the point: the trunk isn't a rectangular prism (sloped seatbacks, wheel wells), boxes leave gaps, and "39.3 cubic feet" is a manufacturer's measurement. Meyer's own Act 3 prompt: "Ask for reasons why our mathematical answer differs from the actual answer."

Routine 2

Which one doesn't belong?

Every one of them can be the answer. Find a reason for each.

A

9

B

16

C

25

D

43

More sets at talkingmathwithkids.com/wodb · Christopher Danielson

Think time, then turn and talk. Push for a reason for EACH one. 9: the only one-digit number. 16: the only even number. 25: the only multiple of 5. 43: the only one that isn't a perfect square (and the only prime). Revoice precisely: "not a square" vs. "not a square number." More sets to pull from live: talkingmathwithkids.com/wodb.

Routine 3 · Would You Rather?

Would you rather use…

Option A

One 70% off coupon

Option B

40% off, then 20% off, then 10% off

Commit to a side before you calculate. Then justify it with mathematics.

wouldyourathermath.com · John Stevens, after Robert Kaplinsky

Hands up for A or B BEFORE anyone calculates. Many choose B because 40+20+10 = 70 "and you get three coupons." Then let them work it out. B keeps 0.6 × 0.8 × 0.9 = 0.432 of the price: 56.8% off. A wins. Good follow-ups: Does the order of the three coupons matter? (No: multiplication is commutative.) What single coupon equals B? (56.8%.) The move to name: committing first makes the calculation matter to them.

When a routine opens a lesson

Warm it up.
Don't give it away.

After the warm-up, could a student still show you something of their own on the task?

The design principle from TCE 318P. A good opening routine warms up the SAME mathematics the main task explores and lets you hear students' entry thinking, but it withholds the move the task is meant to reveal. Example: if today's task is unit rate, Day 50 is a great warm-up. A worked unit-price example would give the task away. Invite one quick "what would give it away?" for the coupon task.

School math vs. research math

School math

The answer already exists, in the teacher's key.

You check your work by comparing it to the answer.

A wrong answer is a mistake.

Research math

Nobody knows the answer. Not your teacher, not the internet, not AI.

You gather evidence, make claims, and hunt for counterexamples.

A counterexample is progress.

Are you suggesting that middle grades students engage in math research, in addition to (or in lieu of) school math?

Why yes, I am!

Why would I do that?

Read the question aloud as if a student asked it, answer "Why yes, I am!", then turn the last question back to the room and take a few answers before moving on. Ideas to draw out if they don't come up: students almost never get to do mathematics where the answer doesn't exist yet; when no key exists, they have to decide what counts as evidence, how much to trust a pattern, and whether examples ever add up to a proof; and in a world where AI can produce answers on demand, a problem nobody has solved is one it can't simply hand them. Plant for the teacher-hat discussion: "How many cases would convince you? Would any number of cases be a proof?"

An unsolved problem

Try 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5. Apply the rule. Repeat.

What happened to your number?

If n is even

n ÷ 2

If n is odd

3n − 1

By hand, before anyone opens the applet. Pairs can split the five numbers. For you: 1 and 2 bounce back and forth (1 → 2 → 1). 3 goes 3 → 8 → 4 → 2 → 1 and joins them. 4 → 2 → 1. But 5 makes its own loop: 5 → 14 → 7 → 20 → 10 → 5. Two different endings from five tiny numbers is the hook. Ask: "Is that all the loops there are?" Hand out the Orbit Explorer task card now. Don't name the problem; the reveal comes after move 3.

Four moves: predict before you press

1 · Go bigger

Pick a number from 21 to 50. Predict, then run it.

2 · Pool the class data

Where did everyone end up? How many different loops?

3 · Change one thing

Move b to +1 and set max steps to 200. Now try 27.

4 · Conjecture

“I conjecture that…” and one “I wonder…”

ohiomathteacher.github.io/orbit-explorer · pairs, about 25 minutes

Orbit Explorer opens on this rule (m = 3, b = −1, d = 2), so moves 1 and 2 need no slider changes. For you (don't give it away): every start from 21 to 50 ends in one of three loops. The 1 ↔ 2 loop catches 9 of them, the 5-loop catches 8, and a loop of 18 numbers through 17 (17, 50, 25, 74, 37, 110, 55, 164, 82, 41, 122, 61, 182, 91, 272, 136, 68, 34) catches 13. Pooling the class data to find "exactly three loops, so far" is the research moment. Ask "how many cases would convince you?" For move 3, max steps MUST go up: at the default 50, 27 stops partway at 1,132. At 200, it shows the whole climb to 9,232 and the fall to 1 (111 steps). Then go to the reveal.

The reveal

That +1 version has a name: the Collatz conjecture.

Does every starting number end at 1? Nobody knows. It has been open since 1937.

And your 3n − 1 question, whether every number lands in one of the loops you found, is unsolved too.

Let this land: they just did research on a famous open problem without knowing it, which is exactly the point of withholding the name. Computers have checked the +1 version past 10²⁰ starting numbers; for the −1 version, only three loops are known, and no one has proved there are no others. Then move to the teacher-hat questions.

Primes · Grades 6–8

Goldbach's Conjecture

Every even number bigger than 2 can be made by adding two primes.

Ask your students

Which even numbers can be made the most ways?

Page ↗ · One-pager (PDF) ↗

10 = 3 + 7 = 5 + 5; 100 works six ways. Goldbach wrote it to Euler in 1742; computers have checked into the billions of billions. Each dot is an even number and how many ways it splits; the orange band is multiples of 3.

Primes · Grades 6–8

Twin Prime Conjecture

Primes that are just 2 apart, like 11 and 13, keep showing up forever.

Ask your students

Look at the number between each twin pair: 4, 6, 12, 18, 30… What do you notice?

Page ↗ · One-pager (PDF) ↗

After 3 and 5, every number between twins is a multiple of 6. Since 2013 we know primes keep coming within 246 of each other forever; getting that down to 2 is the open part.

Primes and squares · Grades 7–9

Legendre's Conjecture

Between any two perfect squares that sit next to each other, there's always a prime.

Ask your students

Which strip has the fewest primes? Could one ever have none?

Page ↗ · One-pager (PDF) ↗

Between 4 and 9: 5 and 7. Between 16 and 25: 17, 19, 23. One of four prime problems listed as out of reach in 1912; all four are still open. Proved cousin: there's always a prime between any number and its double.

Primes and subtraction · Grades 5–8

Gilbreath's Conjecture

List the primes. Subtract neighbors to make a new row. Keep going. Every new row starts with 1.

Ask your students

Start with a different list, like 1, 2, 4, 8, 16. Does it still work?

Page ↗ · One-pager (PDF) ↗

Only subtraction. Norman Gilbreath noticed it in 1958 doodling on a napkin; computers have checked hundreds of billions of rows. Great whole-class build on the board.

Addition · Grades 4–7

The 196 Problem

Reverse a number and add. Repeat until it reads the same both ways. Does 196 ever get there?

Ask your students

89 and 98 take the same number of steps. Why?

Page ↗ · One-pager (PDF) ↗

57 + 75 = 132, 132 + 231 = 363: done in 2 steps. 89 takes 24 steps. Computers have followed 196 for hundreds of millions of digits with no palindrome. Only addition, so it fits the youngest students.

Fractions · Grades 5–8

Erdős–Straus Conjecture

Any fraction with 4 on top can be split into three fractions with 1 on top.

Ask your students

Try 4/9. What's your first move?

Page ↗ · One-pager (PDF) ↗

4/5 = 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/20. For 4/9, the greedy move (take away the biggest unit fraction that fits) gives 1/3 + 1/9, only two pieces. Splitting 1/9 into 1/10 + 1/90 makes three. Egyptian scribes wrote fractions this way almost 4,000 years ago.

Factors · Grades 5–8

Odd Perfect Numbers

A number is perfect when its factors add up to it, like 6 = 1 + 2 + 3. Is any perfect number odd?

Ask your students

Can an odd number's factors ever add to more than it? Try 945.

Page ↗ · One-pager (PDF) ↗

28 = 1 + 2 + 4 + 7 + 14. About fifty perfect numbers are known, all even. 945 is the smallest odd number whose factors add to more than itself, a great surprise for students who conjecture "never."

Rates and motion · Grades 7–10

Lonely Runner Conjecture

Runners start together on a round track, at different speeds. Will each one, at some moment, be far from everyone else?

Ask your students

Act it out with three speeds. Freeze! Who's lonely?

Page ↗ · One-pager (PDF) ↗

"Far" depends on how many runners: half a lap for 2, a third for 3, a quarter for 4. Proved for up to seven runners; for eight or more, nobody knows. Rates and fractions of a lap in disguise.
